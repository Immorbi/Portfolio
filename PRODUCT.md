# Product

## Register

brand

## Platform

web

## Users

Art directors, founders, and hiring leads who are deciding whether to bring a designer onto their team. They arrive from a link — a CV, a LinkedIn message, a recommendation — and skim before they commit to reading. Three groups matter roughly equally: cultural projects and art institutions (residencies, galleries, independent spaces, publications), product and tech companies that need interface work, and studios or agencies hiring into a team. The job they're doing is not "admire a portfolio"; it is deciding, quickly, whether this person is worth a conversation.

## Product Purpose

The portfolio for Maria Danilova, a multidisciplinary designer based in Tbilisi with 6+ years across brand identity, editorial systems, web and UI/UX, and digital culture. It exists to convert a cold visit into a remote role or a long contract. Success is an inbound message that already assumes the fit — someone writing because they have a team in mind, not asking whether she takes work.

## Positioning

She carries one recognisable hand across the whole thing — identity, interface, illustration, and the physical object — and she builds it herself rather than handing off a mockup.

## Conversion & proof

- Primary CTA: contact for a remote role or long contract, via email and the CV download in the sidebar.
- Secondary CTA, for visitors not ready to write: go deeper into a single case and read it end to end.
- The line a visitor remembers after 10 seconds: *working with her will be interesting* — there is taste and a point of view here, the work will not be boring.
- Belief ladder: this person has real range → the range is one voice, not scatter → she ships finished things, not decks → she is worth writing to. *(Derived from the answers above rather than asked directly; correct it if the order is wrong.)*
- Proof on hand: outcome numbers already stated on the homepage — The Gallery, 6 exhibitions and a community grown to 10,000+ followers; DIRTALK, content shares grown 2x in the first three months. Named clients and venues carry the rest: Helikon Art Center, Murabex, DIRTALK, Yufiko, The Gallery.

## Brand Personality

Lively, playful, unexpected. The voice is a person with taste talking plainly about her own work, not a studio writing about itself in the third person. Warmth and humour are allowed; earnest self-seriousness is not. Note the current tension worth designing against: the case pages are disciplined and quiet while the personality lives almost entirely in the side rooms — the draggable archive, the ceramics, the tools. The playfulness should reach the main path, not hide in the corners.

## Anti-references

The templated Behance portfolio: identical preview cards in a uniform grid, mockups floating on gradients, project titles with no argument under them, and no trace of whose site it is. If a visitor could swap the name at the top and notice nothing, it has failed.

## Design Principles

**Range that reads as one hand.** Brand, interface, illustration, and ceramics sit on the same site on purpose. Every surface should make the breadth feel deliberate rather than unfocused — the through-line is the person, and it has to be visible.

**Made, not mocked up.** The site, the tools, and the objects are built, not rendered. Prefer the working thing over the picture of the thing: a live interface over a screenshot, a real object over a mockup.

**Earn the skim.** Visitors decide in seconds and arrive mid-scroll. The first screen of any page carries the argument; depth rewards the ones who stay but never gates the point.

**Claims carry their outcome.** Where a number exists, it appears next to the work. No adjectives standing in for results.

**Personality on the main path.** The odd, hand-made corners are the differentiator against the templated portfolio. They belong in the visitor's way, not filed behind a secondary link.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

A sensible baseline rather than a certified level: readable contrast, keyboard reachability, descriptive alt text, and honouring `prefers-reduced-motion`. No formal WCAG conformance target is claimed. Motion-heavy surfaces — the auto-scrolling case frames, the hover-scrub on the ceramics, the draggable archive — each need a still, usable fallback, since they are the places this baseline is easiest to lose.
